How to customise the Windows desktop with shells, docks and live wallpapers

What Windows lets you change on its own, what a desktop shell adds on top, what live wallpapers really cost, and how to try one without getting stuck.

By Hasnain Butt Akhtar, founder and developer · 10 November 2026

The Windows desktop is one of the few things you look at all day and almost never change. A lot can be done without installing anything, and a desktop shell can go much further. The trick is knowing what each layer changes, what it costs your PC, and how to get back to normal when you want to.

Start with what Windows already does

Before installing anything, it is worth spending five minutes in Settings, because the built-in options cover more than most people realise.

  1. Open Settings, then Personalisation. Background lets you pick a picture, a solid colour, a slideshow from a folder, or Windows Spotlight, which changes the picture every day.
  2. Under Colours, switch between light and dark mode and choose an accent colour, or let Windows take one from your wallpaper.
  3. Under Taskbar, set the icons to the centre or the left, hide the ones you never use and turn on automatic hiding if you want the whole screen.
  4. Under Themes, save the combination so you can switch back to it later.

If a clean, calm desktop is all you are after, that may be enough. What Windows does not do is move, react to what your PC is doing, or put live information on the desktop itself. That is where the next layer comes in.

What a desktop shell actually is

A desktop shell is an app that draws its own desktop over the Windows one. Depending on the shell, that means a dock instead of the taskbar, widgets that sit on the wallpaper, a launcher you open from the keyboard, and a wallpaper that moves. Windows is still running underneath: your apps, files and settings are exactly where they were. The shell is a new front room, not a new house.

That distinction matters, because it tells you what to look for. A good shell sits on top of Windows and steps aside cleanly. A poor one fights with it.

Docks, widgets and launchers

A dock replaces or sits beside the taskbar. The useful ones show your pinned apps and every open window, with a preview when you hover. If you rely on the taskbar's system tray for volume, network and battery, check the dock has its own, or you will find yourself hunting for them.

Widgets put information on the wallpaper: a clock, the weather, how busy your processor and memory are, what is playing. They are most useful on a second screen, where a glance tells you whether something is hogging the machine without opening Task Manager.

A launcher opens from a key press and finds apps and files as you type. Once you are used to one, it is faster than any menu, and it is the feature people miss most when they go back.

What live wallpapers really cost

A moving wallpaper is drawn continuously by your graphics card, a little like a very simple game running behind your work. On a desktop PC with a dedicated graphics card the cost is small. On a laptop, especially on battery, it is noticeable: more power use, a warmer machine and a fan that runs more often.

A well made shell keeps that cost down by pausing the wallpaper when an app fills the screen, when a game is running or when the shell is hidden. If a live wallpaper keeps drawing behind a full-screen game, you are paying for frames nobody sees. Look for a frame rate setting, a calm or reduced motion option, and an automatic pause.

Choosing one you can trust

A desktop shell sees more of your PC than most apps you install: every window you open, the apps you use, often your clipboard. That is fine, as long as none of it leaves the machine. Before you commit to one, check:

  • It gives the desktop back. There should be a clear way to return to the normal Windows desktop and taskbar, and the taskbar should come back on its own if the shell closes.
  • It asks first. Hiding the taskbar or starting with Windows should be your choice, not something that happens on install.
  • It needs no account. A desktop has no reason to sign in anywhere.
  • It says what goes online. Weather and maps need a connection. Nothing else should.
  • It uninstalls cleanly. Removing it from Settings, Apps should leave Windows exactly as it was.

Trying one safely

Use the free trial on a normal working day rather than a quiet evening, because that is when you find out whether the dock and launcher suit the way you work. Learn the exit before you need it: the tray icon, the menu option or the key that returns you to Windows. Leave start with Windows switched off until you are sure you want to keep it.

If something goes wrong

What you seeWhat to do
The taskbar is missing after a shell closedPress Ctrl, Shift and Esc to open Task Manager, find Windows Explorer, and choose Restart.
The fan runs more than usualLower the wallpaper frame rate, choose a calm mode, or use a still picture on battery.
Games feel slowerCheck the wallpaper pauses when a game is full screen. If it has no such option, close the shell while you play.
Widgets jump around on a second screenSet the screen layout in Settings, System, Display first, then place the widgets again.
You want the old desktop back for goodTurn off start with Windows in the shell, or uninstall it from Settings, Apps.

The short version: get the built-in options right first, then try a shell if you want a desktop that moves and tells you something. Choose one that pauses when you play, asks before it hides the taskbar, works without an account and hands the desktop back the moment you ask.
