What 7B, Q4 and the rest of a local AI model name mean

Model files come with names like llama-3-8b-instruct-q4_k_m. Every part of that tells you something useful, including whether it will run on your machine.

By Hasnain Butt Akhtar, founder and developer · 5 September 2026

Downloading a local AI model means choosing between files with names that look like registration plates. They are not arbitrary. Each part answers a question you need answered, and the two that matter most tell you whether the thing will fit in your machine at all.

Reading the name

Take a typical example, llama-3-8b-instruct-q4_k_m.gguf, and break it apart.

PartWhat it tells you
llama-3Which family and version the model comes from
8bEight billion parameters. This is the size of the model.
instructTuned to follow instructions and hold a conversation
q4_k_mCompressed to roughly four bits per parameter, medium quality variant
ggufThe file format, used by most desktop AI applications

The parameter count

The number followed by B is billions of parameters, and it is the closest thing to a measure of how capable the model is. A 70B model knows more and reasons better than an 8B one from the same family.

It also determines how much memory the file needs, and the arithmetic is simple. At full precision each parameter takes two bytes, so an 8B model needs about 16 GB before anything else is loaded. That is why models are almost always compressed before people run them at home.

Quantisation, which is the part that makes it possible

Quantisation stores each parameter using fewer bits. Instead of two bytes per parameter you use roughly half a byte, and the file shrinks by about four times. Some accuracy is lost, and the interesting part is how little.

Here is the practical guide for a model with 7 or 8 billion parameters:

LabelRough sizeQuality
Q8About 8 GBEffectively identical to the original
Q6About 6 GBVery close, hard to tell apart in normal use
Q5About 5 GBSlight loss, still very good
Q4About 4 GBThe usual choice. Noticeably compressed but reliable.
Q3About 3 GBQuality starts dropping in ways you will notice
Q2About 2.5 GBUsually not worth it

The letters after the number, such as K_M or K_S, are variants of the same level. Medium is the sensible default; small saves a little more space for a little more loss.

The rule worth remembering

Given a fixed amount of memory, a larger model compressed more usually beats a smaller model compressed less.

If you have about 8 GB to work with, a 13B model at Q4 will generally outperform a 7B model at Q8, even though the 7B version is technically more faithful to its original. The extra capability from the bigger model matters more than the extra precision.

This holds down to about Q4. Below that the losses start to outweigh the benefit, so Q3 and Q2 are worth avoiding unless nothing else fits.

Working out what your machine can run

Two numbers decide this: your graphics card memory, and your system memory.

Running entirely on the graphics card is much faster, and needs the whole model to fit in graphics memory with about a gigabyte spare. Running on the processor using system memory is slower but works with far larger models, since ordinary memory is cheaper and more plentiful. Most software will split the model between the two, putting as much as fits on the graphics card and the rest in system memory, which lands somewhere in the middle for speed.

  1. 6 to 8 GB of graphics memory. A 7B or 8B model at Q4 fits comfortably and runs quickly. This is the sweet spot for most home machines.
  2. 12 to 16 GB of graphics memory. A 13B or 14B model at Q4, or an 8B at higher quality.
  3. 24 GB or more. A 30B class model at Q4 becomes practical.
  4. No suitable graphics card. Run on the processor with 16 GB or more of system memory. A 7B model at Q4 works and produces text at reading speed rather than instantly, which is perfectly usable for writing and summarising.

Leave headroom. The figures above are the model alone, and the conversation itself also occupies memory, increasing as it gets longer.

The other words you will see

Instruct or chat means the model has been trained to follow instructions and answer questions. This is what you want for almost any normal use. A model without that label is a base model, which continues text rather than responding to it, and is mainly of interest to people building on top of it.

Context length, often quoted as 8K, 32K or 128K, is how much text the model can consider at once, including everything said so far. Longer contexts use more memory, sometimes considerably, so a model advertised with a very large context may need more than its file size suggests if you actually use it.

GGUF is the file format most desktop applications expect, and it is designed to run well on ordinary hardware including processors. Safetensors files are the full precision originals, generally larger and aimed at people doing development work rather than running a model to use it.

If you want a starting point rather than a decision tree: an 8B instruct model at Q4 in GGUF format runs on most machines made in the last few years, needs about 5 GB of space, and is good enough for writing, summarising and answering questions. Start there, see whether it is capable enough for what you need, and only then work out whether your hardware can take something larger.
