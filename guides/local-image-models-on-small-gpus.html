Running local image models on a 4 GB or 6 GB GPU

Why your card runs out of memory, what actually fixes it, and the settings that matter in the order they matter.

By Hasnain Butt Akhtar, founder and developer · 4 September 2026

Almost every "CUDA out of memory" on a small card comes down to three numbers most tools never show you: how big the model is, how many pixels you asked for, and whether anything is being kept in memory that does not need to be. Fix them in that order and a 4 GB card is a perfectly serviceable image generator.

Where the memory actually goes

A diffusion model does not load as one lump. Three things compete for the same VRAM, and only the first is the number people quote.

The weights. These are fixed and predictable. An SD 1.5-class UNet is about 860 million parameters, which at half precision is roughly 1.7 GB. An SDXL-class UNet is 2.6 billion parameters, or about 5.2 GB at the same precision — before the text encoders, which on SDXL are another 1.4 GB or so because there are two of them. That arithmetic alone tells you why SDXL is painful on a 6 GB card and impossible on a 4 GB one, and no clever setting changes it.

The activations. This is the part that surprises people, because it scales with resolution far worse than you would expect. The model works on a latent image one eighth the size of the output, and the attention layers compare every latent position with every other one. At 512×512 that is a 64×64 latent, or 4,096 positions. At 1024×1024 it is 128×128, or 16,384 positions — four times the pixels, but sixteen times the attention matrix. Doubling the side of your image does not double the memory. It quadruples the pixel count and, in a naive implementation, multiplies the peak attention allocation by sixteen.

The decode. Once denoising finishes, the VAE turns the latent back into pixels, and it does that in one pass at full resolution. On a big image this single step can spike over a gigabyte on its own. If your generation gets to 100% and then crashes, this is what killed it, not the model.

What each setting really does

Most tools expose the same handful of switches under different names. They are not interchangeable, and they do not cost the same.

SettingWhat it doesCosts you
Half precision (fp16 / bf16)Stores weights in 16 bits instead of 32, halving the model's footprintNothing visible. Use it always.
Memory-efficient attentionComputes attention in blocks so the full matrix never exists at once — kills the quadratic spikeNothing, and it is often faster
Attention slicingOlder, cruder version of the above: processes attention heads in sequencePerhaps 10–20% speed
VAE tilingDecodes the final image in overlapping tiles instead of one passA little speed; very occasionally a faint seam
Model offloadingKeeps parts of the model in system RAM and moves them to the GPU only when neededReal speed — often 2–3× slower
Sequential offloadingThe aggressive version: one submodule on the GPU at a timeA lot of speed. Last resort.

The order matters. Half precision and memory-efficient attention are free, so they should already be on. VAE tiling is nearly free and solves the crash-at-100% problem specifically. Offloading is the only one that genuinely hurts, so it belongs last, and only if the model is simply too large for the card.

The setting nobody mentions: resolution

Generating far outside a model's training resolution does not merely cost memory, it costs quality. An SD 1.5-class model was trained at 512×512. Ask it for 1024×1024 directly and you will frequently get duplicated heads, a second torso, or a figure with three arms — the model has no concept of a composition that large, so it tiles what it does know. People often blame the prompt for this. The prompt is fine.

The fix is the same thing professional pipelines do anyway: generate at the model's native size, then enlarge. A 512×512 generation followed by a 2× upscale gives you a coherent 1024×1024 image using a fraction of the memory, and it will beat a direct 1024 generation from the same model almost every time. On an SDXL-class model, the native size is 1024, and the same logic applies from there.

A working recipe, by card

  1. 4 GB. SD 1.5-class model, half precision, memory-efficient attention on, VAE tiling on, batch size 1, generate at 512×512 (or 512×768 for portraits), then upscale 2×. This runs comfortably and does not need offloading. Do not attempt SDXL here; it is not a settings problem.
  2. 6 GB. Everything above, and SDXL becomes possible with model offloading enabled — expect it to be considerably slower. Try it, time it, and be honest about whether the result justifies the wait. For a lot of work, SD 1.5 at native resolution plus a good upscaler finishes sooner and looks better.
  3. 8 GB and up. SDXL at 1024 without offloading, batch size 1. The advice stops being interesting here, which is the point at which you can ignore most of this page.

Two Windows-specific traps

Your card is not the only thing using it. A browser with hardware acceleration on can hold several hundred megabytes to a gigabyte of VRAM, and on a 4 GB card that is the difference between working and not. Close Chrome or Edge before a long run. It is an unglamorous fix that resolves a surprising share of out-of-memory reports.

Windows will not always crash — it will crawl. Modern drivers spill past dedicated VRAM into system RAM rather than failing outright, so instead of an error you get a generation that takes eight minutes instead of forty seconds. Open Task Manager, go to Performance, select your GPU, and watch the two figures separately: Dedicated GPU memory and Shared GPU memory. If shared memory is climbing during a run, you are not out of memory in the sense that anything will tell you about — you are quietly running the model over the PCIe bus. Lower the resolution or enable tiling until shared memory stays flat.

How to diagnose it in one pass

When something fails, the timing tells you which of the three consumers ran out.

SymptomCauseFix
Fails before the first stepWeights do not fitSmaller model, or enable offloading
Fails partway through denoisingAttention spike at this resolutionMemory-efficient attention; lower resolution
Reaches 100%, then failsVAE decodeEnable VAE tiling
Completes, but takes minutesSpilling into shared memoryFree VRAM, or reduce resolution until shared stays flat
Completes, but the anatomy is wrongGenerating above native resolutionGenerate native, then upscale

None of this is specific to any one application. The three consumers — weights, activations, decode — are properties of how diffusion models work, so the same reasoning applies whichever tool you run them in, and the same five symptoms map to the same five fixes. If a tool does not let you control tiling and attention, that is worth knowing before you conclude your card is the problem.
