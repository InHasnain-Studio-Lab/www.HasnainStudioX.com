What Windows collects, and which settings are worth changing

A realistic pass through the privacy settings: what each one does, what it does not do, and why the debloating scripts are a bad trade.

By Hasnain Butt Akhtar, founder and developer · 6 September 2026

Windows privacy advice tends to arrive in two flavours: nothing to worry about, or a script that removes half the operating system. The useful version is in between. About twenty minutes of settings changes covers most of what is worth covering, and it is worth knowing what those changes actually achieve.

Diagnostic data, and the part you cannot turn off

Windows sends information about how the system is running back to Microsoft. It comes in two levels, and the distinction matters.

Required diagnostic data covers device configuration, whether updates installed successfully, crashes and errors. On the consumer editions of Windows this cannot be switched off. Enterprise editions can reduce it further, but Home and Pro cannot. Anyone telling you otherwise is describing a registry change that Windows ignores or reverses.

Optional diagnostic data adds which apps you use and for how long, browsing activity in Microsoft's own browser, and more detailed error reports. This one you can turn off, and it is the single most worthwhile change on this page.

Find it in Settings, Privacy and security, then Diagnostics and feedback. Switch off the optional data. On the same page, turn off tailored experiences, which is the setting that uses your diagnostic data to personalise tips and advertisements. Below that, there is a delete button for the diagnostic data already collected, which is worth pressing once.

The rest of the list, in order of usefulness

  1. Advertising ID. In Settings, Privacy and security, General. This is a unique identifier that apps can use to link your activity across them for advertising. Turning it off resets it and stops apps reading it. There is no downside.
  2. Activity history. Same area of settings. Windows records which apps and files you have opened. Turning it off costs you the timeline feature, which most people never use.
  3. App permissions. Still in Privacy and security, there is a list covering location, camera, microphone, contacts, and more. Each one shows exactly which apps have asked for access. Going through camera, microphone and location and removing anything that has no business with them takes five minutes and is the most concrete thing on this list.
  4. Search permissions. Windows search sends what you type in the Start menu to the web by default. If you only want it to find things on your own machine, turn off the cloud content search and web results. It also makes the Start menu noticeably faster.
  5. Location history. If you leave location on for a laptop you may want to find, clear the stored history periodically. The button is on the same page.

The account question

Windows pushes you towards signing in with a Microsoft account, and it genuinely does useful things: settings sync across machines, device recovery, and BitLocker keys backed up so you are not locked out of your own drive.

A local account keeps everything on the machine and links nothing to an online identity. The cost is real though. You lose settings sync, you lose the ability to find the device, and critically, if you use drive encryption you become entirely responsible for storing the recovery key. People lose those keys and lose everything on the drive.

There is no universally right answer. If you choose a local account, write your encryption recovery key down and keep it somewhere physical, today rather than eventually.

Why the removal scripts are a bad idea

There is a whole genre of tools promising to strip Windows down by removing components and disabling services. They are popular, and they cause a steady stream of problems that surface weeks later and are very hard to trace back.

The typical failures are Windows Update refusing to install, the Microsoft Store not working, printing or Bluetooth quietly breaking, and features returning after a major update in a half-configured state. By the time any of it appears, you have long forgotten which script you ran, and the usual fix is reinstalling Windows.

Everything genuinely worth turning off is available in the settings app, supported, reversible, and survives updates. That is a much better trade than saving a few hundred megabytes and gaining an unstable system.

The part most people have backwards

For the average person, Windows is not the main source of tracking. The browser is.

Advertising networks build profiles from what you visit, and that happens in the browser regardless of which operating system it is running on. Ten minutes spent on browser settings will do more for your privacy than every setting above put together.

Three things worth doing there: block third party cookies, install a reputable content blocker, and choose a search engine that does not build a profile. If you want to go further, the browser's own tracking protection set to its strictest level is more effective than most people expect, though it occasionally breaks a site and needs an exception.

Realistic expectations

None of this makes you invisible, and it is not meant to. What it does is stop the casual, automatic collection that happens by default: the advertising identifier following you between apps, the optional telemetry, the app with no reason to use your microphone doing it anyway.

Worth repeating one point, because it is the one that costs people real money and real files. If you turn off your Microsoft account or move to a local account, find your BitLocker recovery key first and store it somewhere outside the machine. A locked drive with no key is not recoverable by anyone, including Microsoft.
