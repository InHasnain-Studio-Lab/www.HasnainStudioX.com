How to write better AI image prompts

Subject first, one layer at a time, and how to finish the picture

By Hasnain Butt Akhtar, Hasnain Studio X · 23 August 2026 · 6 minute read

On this page

  1. Write the subject before the style
  2. Add one layer at a time
  3. Be specific about things, vague about quality
  4. Change one thing, keep the rest
  5. Generate several, then choose
  6. Save what works
  7. Generating is not finishing
  8. The habits worth keeping

Most disappointing results come from the same habit: writing one long prompt, not liking what comes back, rewriting the whole thing, and starting over. There is a method that works better, and it takes less effort than the rewriting does.

Write the subject before the style

Start with what is in the frame and nothing else. One clear sentence: who or what, doing what, where.

A snow leopard standing on a rock ledge at dawn.

Generate that. It will look plain, and that is the point — you now know the subject is understood. Almost every prompt that produces something unrecognisable failed at this step, buried under adjectives that were competing for attention.

The temptation is to write the finished vision immediately. Resist it for one generation. Thirty seconds spent confirming the subject saves you twenty attempts at fixing the wrong thing.

Add one layer at a time

Now build, in this order, generating after each addition:

  1. Light. The single biggest change you can make. Low golden light from the left, long shadows. Nothing transforms an image faster.
  2. Framing. How close, and from where. Close crop, slightly below eye level.
  3. Medium. What kind of picture this is. Photograph gives you something very different from oil painting or pencil study, and it anchors everything after it.
  4. Mood and palette. Last, and lightly. Cold blues, quiet.

Adding one layer per generation means you always know which word did what. Add four at once and you learn nothing — you have a different image and no idea which change produced it.

Be specific about things, vague about quality

Words like beautiful, stunning, masterpiece and 8K do very little. They describe your opinion of the result rather than the result itself.

Concrete detail does the work:

WeakStrong
beautiful lightingsingle window light from the left, deep shadow on the right
highly detailedfrost on the whiskers, individual hairs catching the light
professional photoshallow depth of field, background fully soft
vibrant colourswarm amber and deep teal, nothing in between
epic scenethe figure small in the frame, mountains filling the top two thirds

If a word could describe a thousand different images, it is not earning its place.

Change one thing, keep the rest

Once something is close, stop rewriting and start adjusting. Keep the prompt and change a single element — the light, the crop, one adjective. Generate. Compare.

This is how you build an instinct for what actually moves the image, and it is much faster than it sounds, because you are no longer starting over each time. Three targeted adjustments usually beat thirty rewrites.

Keep the ones that nearly worked, too. A version you rejected at step three is often the better base once you have learned something at step six.

Generate several, then choose

The same prompt does not produce the same image twice. Once a prompt is working, run it several times rather than once, and pick from the results.

This is where working on your own machine changes how you work. There is no per-image cost and no credit balance, so generating twelve and keeping one is simply how you work rather than an expensive decision. Most people's best images come from a batch they nearly did not bother running.

Save what works

When you find a combination that consistently gives you the look you want — the lighting phrasing, the medium, the palette — write it down and reuse it. That is a house style, and it is the difference between occasional good results and a body of work that looks like it belongs together.

Generating is not finishing

A generated image is a starting point. The gap between something you like and something you would actually publish is usually a few minutes of work afterwards.

  • Faces repay attention more than anything else, because people look at them first. HSX FotoTensor is built around portraits and face enhancement.
  • Cleanup and adjustment — tone, colour, removing the one distracting element. HSX GlowCraft handles this with on-device AI tools.
  • Size and export. Print, web and social all want different things. Getting this right at export is easier than fixing it later.
  • Motion. A still can become a short clip. HSX QuantumGen AI Studio, HSX DreamVivid AI and HSX InfiniteGen Studio all produce clips as well as images.

The habits worth keeping

  1. Subject first, alone, before any styling.
  2. One layer at a time, generating between each.
  3. Concrete nouns over quality adjectives.
  4. Adjust one element rather than rewriting.
  5. Batch and choose, rather than judging a single result.
  6. Save the combinations that work, and reuse them.
  7. Finish the image after you generate it.
