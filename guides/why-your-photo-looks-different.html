Why your photo looks different on every screen

You edited it carefully, then it arrived on someone else's phone looking washed out. Here is what changes between screens, and what you can control.

By Hasnain Butt Akhtar, founder and developer · 6 September 2026

You spend twenty minutes getting a photograph exactly right, send it to someone, and they see something noticeably different. Nothing has gone wrong with the file. Several separate things change between your screen and theirs, and only some of them are yours to fix.

What is actually changing

Brightness, most of all. This is the biggest single factor and the one people think about least. Screens vary enormously in how bright they are set, and a phone outdoors adjusts itself constantly. An image that looks correctly exposed on a dim laptop looks blown out on a bright phone. There is no setting in your file that changes this.

The range of colours the screen can show. Not all screens can display the same colours. A basic office monitor covers a modest range, a good modern phone covers considerably more, and a professional display more again. The same red can be vivid on one and muted on another simply because one screen cannot physically produce it.

How saturated the screen makes things. Phone manufacturers deliberately boost colour, in a vivid or adaptive display mode, because people prefer it in a shop. Your carefully judged skin tones arrive slightly more orange than you left them.

Whether anyone told the screen what the colours mean. This is the one you can control, and it is where most avoidable problems come from.

The part you can control

Numbers in an image file do not mean anything on their own. A pixel described as 200, 60, 60 is only a particular red if you know which set of colours those numbers refer to. That information is the colour profile, and it travels inside the file as a small tag.

When the tag is missing, software has to guess, and it guesses sRGB, which is the common standard the internet was built on. If your image was actually saved in a wider range such as Adobe RGB or Display P3, that guess is wrong, and the result is the classic complaint: colours that look flat, dull and slightly wrong compared with what you exported.

The fix is simple and it is the single most useful thing on this page. Export in sRGB for anything going on the internet, and make sure the profile is embedded. Most editing software has both options in the export dialogue. Wider colour spaces are genuinely useful while you are editing, and for printing, but they should be converted to sRGB on the way out.

Getting your own screen closer to right

You cannot control other people's screens, so the goal is to make yours neutral enough that your edits are sensible starting points rather than corrections for your own display's quirks.

  1. Turn off adaptive colour features while editing. Night light, blue light reduction and automatic colour temperature all shift the display warmer, usually in the evening. Editing under them produces photographs that look strangely cold to everyone else. Turn them off before colour work.
  2. Set a sensible brightness. Comfortable in a normally lit room, not at maximum. Editing on a screen at full brightness produces images that are too dark for everyone else, because you compensated for a screen brighter than theirs.
  3. Check your monitor's picture mode. Many displays ship in a vivid or dynamic mode that oversaturates everything. A standard or sRGB mode is a much better starting point.
  4. Consider a calibrator if this is your work. A hardware calibrator measures your screen and builds a correction profile. It costs about as much as a decent lens filter and is the only way to make a display genuinely accurate. For casual use it is overkill. For anyone selling prints it pays for itself.

Why prints look darker

This surprises people every time, and it is not a fault. A screen makes its own light and shines it at you. Paper reflects whatever light is in the room. Paper simply cannot produce the brightness range a backlit screen can, so a photograph with deep shadows that look rich on screen turns into a dark mass on paper.

The practical answer is to prepare prints slightly brighter than looks right on screen, particularly in the shadows, and to judge the result under decent light. Good printing services publish profiles for their paper that let editing software preview the effect before you commit, which is worth using for anything expensive.

The other half is the paper itself. A matte paper has less contrast than a gloss one, and both differ from your screen. If you print regularly with one service, one test print teaches you more about the difference than any amount of theory.

A quick checklist before you publish

CheckWhy
Exported in sRGB with the profile embeddedPrevents the dull, flat look on other screens
Night light and blue light filters off while editingStops a warm screen pushing your edits cold
Monitor in a standard rather than vivid modeKeeps saturation judgements honest
Viewed on your phone before publishingCatches images that are too dark, which is the most common problem
Viewed on a second screen if you have oneAnything that looks wrong on both is genuinely wrong

You are aiming for a photograph that looks good across a range of screens rather than perfect on yours. Slightly less extreme editing usually travels better: heavy contrast and deep saturation tend to fall apart on displays that cannot reproduce them, while a moderately edited image looks reasonable almost everywhere.
