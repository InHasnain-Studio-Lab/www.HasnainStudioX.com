An equaliser is the most useful audio control most people never touch, or touch once, drag every slider to the top and decide it makes things worse. Used gently, it can fix the tone of almost any pair of headphones or speakers. The secret is that good equalisation is mostly about taking things away.

What an equaliser actually does

Sound is a mix of low, middle and high frequencies. An equaliser splits that range into bands and lets you turn each one up or down. A ten band equaliser has ten sliders, from the deep bass on the left to the airy top on the right. Each slider is a volume control for one part of the sound, not for the whole thing.

It helps to know roughly what lives where:

Cut before you boost

The most common mistake is boosting the bands you want more of. Every boost makes the whole signal louder, and once it is too loud for the system to carry, the peaks get clipped and you hear distortion, usually as a crackle on bass notes. That is why a strong bass boost so often sounds worse than no boost at all.

Cutting does the same job from the other direction. If the sound is too thin, try cutting the upper middle a little instead of boosting the bass. The balance shifts towards the bass, the overall level drops slightly, and there is no risk of distortion. Turn the main volume up a touch to make up the difference.

If you do boost, keep it modest. A change of two or three decibels is clearly audible. Ten is almost never right.

The volume trap

Louder always sounds better for the first few seconds. That makes it easy to fool yourself: you boost something, the music gets louder, and it seems improved. To judge a change fairly, compare it at the same loudness. Switch the equaliser off and on while adjusting the main volume so both sound equally loud, then decide.

Fixes for common problems

  • Muddy or boxy: cut a little around 250 to 400 Hz.
  • Harsh or tiring: cut a little between 2 and 5 kHz. This is the fix most cheaper headphones need.
  • S sounds hiss: a small cut around 6 to 8 kHz.
  • Thin, no weight: a small lift around 80 to 120 Hz, or a cut in the upper middle.
  • Boomy bass that blurs everything: cut around 100 to 200 Hz rather than the lowest band.
  • Dull and closed in: a gentle lift above 10 kHz.

Make one change at a time, listen to something you know well, and stop when it sounds natural rather than impressive. Natural is what you will still enjoy after an hour.

Presets, and when to use them

Presets such as Rock, Vocal or Bass are just saved slider positions. They can be a useful starting point, but they were set on someone else's headphones in someone else's room. Start from one if it is close, then adjust. A preset that sounds good for films often sounds wrong for music, so it is worth saving one setting for each.

Equalisers on Windows

Windows itself has no general equaliser. Some sound drivers add one in the device's enhancement settings, found from Settings, System, Sound, then your output device, and many drivers do not. Media players often include one, but it only affects what that player plays. A system-wide equaliser app applies to everything at once: games, films, calls and the browser.
